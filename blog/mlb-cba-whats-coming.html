The MLB CBA Is Coming. Here Is What I Am Watching.

Competitive balance, salary structure, and what the next collective bargaining negotiation is really about.

Baseball September 17, 2026

I grew up a Minnesota Twins fan, which means I have spent a meaningful portion of my baseball-watching life watching teams I love get eliminated by teams with payrolls that dwarf ours. I am not bitter about it. It is just the structure of the sport. But heading into a CBA negotiation this offseason, with a Brewers team that has quietly become one of the more interesting stories in baseball making a legitimate playoff run against a Dodgers roster that looks, on paper, like something assembled from a video game, I find myself paying closer attention than usual to what the next agreement might actually address.

I want to be clear that I am writing this as a fan with a law school education, not as someone with any inside knowledge of the negotiations. But the CBA is a labor agreement, and labor agreements are something I have spent time thinking about, and baseball's specific situation is interesting enough to be worth laying out.

What the CBA Is Actually About

The public conversation about MLB labor negotiations usually collapses into a single question: will there be a salary cap? That framing understates the complexity considerably. The Players Association has historically and consistently opposed a hard cap, and with good reason from their perspective. A hard cap limits the ceiling on individual player earnings in a way that does not exist in any other major American professional sport where baseball's antitrust exemption has historically given ownership structural advantages. The union has fought to protect free agency and the ability of elite players to earn market value without an artificial ceiling.

The ownership side has its own set of concerns, and they are not all cynical. Revenue sharing and competitive balance are genuine problems in a sport where the gap between the teams at the top and the teams at the bottom in terms of payroll capacity is as large as it currently is. A team like the Brewers, which has been remarkably well-run and is producing a competitive product on a fraction of what the Dodgers spend, represents the best possible argument for how small-market teams can compete. But it also represents the exception rather than the rule. For every Brewers, there are several franchises where the structural disadvantage is simply too large to overcome with smart management alone.

The Dodgers Problem

The Dodgers are a useful focal point for this conversation not because they have done anything wrong, but because they represent the logical endpoint of the current structure. When a team can absorb the contracts they have absorbed, field the roster they have fielded, and still enter the playoffs as the clear favorite, it raises a legitimate question about whether the current luxury tax framework is functioning as an actual constraint or just a cost of doing business for franchises with sufficient revenue.

The luxury tax was designed to create some competitive balance without the political difficulty of a hard cap. The problem is that for teams with the revenue capacity of the largest markets, the tax threshold is not a meaningful deterrent. It is an expense line. Competing clubs in smaller markets are not facing the same tradeoffs, and the gap compounds over time through player development, free agent acquisition, and the ability to absorb roster risk.

What I Am Watching in the Negotiation

A few specific things will tell me whether the next CBA represents a meaningful shift or another incremental adjustment.

The first is what happens to the pre-arbitration compensation structure. One of the quieter but more significant issues in recent negotiations has been how young players are compensated in their first years of service before they reach arbitration eligibility. The current system suppresses earnings for players during their most physically productive years and creates incentives for service time manipulation. Whatever the next CBA does with pre-arb pay will say a lot about whether ownership is serious about addressing player concerns beyond the headline cap issue.

The second is whether revenue sharing gets restructured in a way that creates actual competitive incentives rather than just transferring money to small-market owners who pocket it rather than reinvesting in the product. Revenue sharing that does not require payroll floors has historically not solved the competitive balance problem. A floor with teeth is different from a floor in name only.

The third is simply whether both sides remember that the last work stoppage cost them real fan engagement at a moment when baseball can afford to lose fans less than it could at any prior point in the sport's history. The 2022 lockout was damaging. Another one would be worse.

The Fan Perspective

I want the Brewers in the playoffs. I want them to compete against the Dodgers and make it interesting. And I want the next CBA to produce a structure where that kind of matchup is the rule rather than a charming anomaly. Whether the negotiation delivers that is a question I will be following closely this offseason, from the perspective of someone who cares about the game and has spent enough time studying labor law to know that these agreements are more complicated than any single headline captures.

Jay Haws is a JD/MBA candidate at Wake Forest University School of Law, Class of 2027. He grew up a Minnesota Twins fan and has not fully recovered from it.
