The New NCAA Eligibility Model Has a Hockey Problem

The age-based framework resolves some things and creates new complications for others. Here is what compliance offices are watching.

Sports Law September 17, 2026

One of the more useful things about working inside a compliance office during a period of significant NCAA rule change is that you get to watch how institutions actually process new regulations in real time. Not the headlines, not the litigation filings, but the practical question: what does this mean for our athletes, and what do we need to explain to them?

The new NCAA eligibility model, which moved away from the traditional class-based framework toward an age-based system, was one of those rules we were paying close attention to this summer as it was proposed, debated, and ultimately enacted. The intent is straightforward enough: standardize eligibility across a more complex and transfer-heavy landscape. But like most rules that look clean in the abstract, it creates real complications at the edges. And the edges are where compliance work actually lives.

The Hockey Problem

The sport that stands out most clearly to me is ice hockey. The developmental pathway for elite hockey players in North America does not follow the same timeline as most other sports. Many players who go on to compete at the Division I level and eventually reach the NHL spend years after high school competing in junior leagues before they ever set foot on a college campus. This is not a detour. It is the standard development track for the sport.

Under the traditional NCAA model, those junior years generally did not count against eligibility as long as certain conditions were met. The class-based structure gave programs and players some flexibility to account for the reality that a 22-year-old freshman hockey player is not unusual. The age-based model changes that calculus. If eligibility is tied to age rather than academic class, the years spent in juniors start to matter differently. Players who follow the standard hockey development path may arrive at their Division I programs with fewer eligible seasons than their peers in other sports who took more conventional routes.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is the kind of question that compliance staff at hockey-playing institutions are working through right now, and it has implications for recruiting, roster planning, and the waiver process.

International Athletes Add Complexity

The international athlete question is a related but distinct problem. Development timelines outside the United States vary significantly by sport and country. An athlete who competed professionally in a foreign league before coming to the United States for college, or who spent years in a national academy system, may find that their age works against them under the new model even if their competitive history would have been treated differently under the old framework.

The NCAA has waiver processes to address individual circumstances, and a significant part of compliance work involves building those waiver submissions and coordinating with other institutions to establish the factual record. But waivers are inherently unpredictable and resource-intensive. A rule that generates a high volume of waiver requests is a rule that is probably not calibrated correctly for the diversity of athletes it governs.

Track, Cross Country, and the Endurance Sports Timeline

A third category worth watching is endurance sports, particularly track and field and cross country. Distance runners often peak later than athletes in power or skill sports. An athlete who was an average collegiate runner at 19 may be a legitimately elite competitor at 24 or 25. The NCAA has historically accommodated some of this through its eligibility framework, but an age-based model that caps participation at a fixed point regardless of athletic development creates tension with the actual physiology of distance running.

This matters less at the individual athlete level than it might seem, because most elite distance runners who are competing seriously in their mid-20s are doing so professionally. But at the margins, particularly for athletes who developed late or took time away from the sport, the new model may cut off eligibility at exactly the point where competitive potential is highest.

What This Looks Like From Inside a Compliance Office

The honest answer is that most of the immediate work this fall has been less about debating the policy merits and more about making sure our athletes and staff understand what the new rules actually say. We updated eligibility forms for the majority of our athletic programs to reflect each student-athlete's remaining eligibility under the new model. Those forms run through academic affairs before they go to the ACC and NCAA. Getting them right matters.

The larger policy questions, whether the model adequately accounts for sport-specific development pathways, whether the waiver process can absorb the volume of edge cases it will generate, and whether international athletes are fairly treated under a framework designed primarily around the American developmental experience, are questions that will get answered through litigation and rule amendments over the next several years. The compliance work is to stay current as those answers develop and make sure the athletes we work with are protected in the meantime.

It is not the most glamorous part of sports law. But it is where the rules actually touch people's careers.
