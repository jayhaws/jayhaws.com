Three Months Into Soccer Fandom. Here Is What I Noticed.

A newcomer's report from the World Cup, with observations on hydration breaks, ticket prices, and where FIFA is taking the sport.

Soccer September 17, 2026

In June I went to my first MLS game and decided, somewhat impulsively, to become a soccer fan. I picked Tottenham, Atletico Madrid, and Borussia Dortmund as my clubs, learned within four days of choosing Spurs that they had been fighting relegation, and concluded that my background as a Minnesota sports fan had prepared me adequately for this kind of suffering.

Three months later, I have watched a significant amount of soccer, tracked World Cup rosters for players from my clubs, attended a World Cup match in Atlanta, and developed opinions I cannot fully justify about refereeing. This is my attempt to document what actually stood out, including the things that had less to do with the sport itself and more to do with the business operating around it.

Atlanta, Spain, and Cape Verde

The Atlanta match was Spain versus Cape Verde, which ended in a draw. I did not have a particular rooting interest in Cape Verde but found myself quietly impressed by them by halftime, which I understand is a common experience at the World Cup when you show up expecting a blowout and get a match instead.

The atmosphere inside a World Cup stadium is difficult to overstate for someone coming from American sports. I have been to MLB games, NFL games, college football. The crowd dynamics are different in a way I did not fully anticipate. There is a continuity to the noise that does not exist when a sport is structured around stoppages. In baseball, the crowd reacts to discrete events. In soccer, the tension builds continuously and the crowd moves with it. It is a different kind of attention the sport demands from you, and I found it more absorbing than I expected.

The Hydration Breaks Were Commercial Breaks

One of the more obvious things about the World Cup, once you notice it, is that the mandatory hydration breaks introduced for matches in extreme heat conditions functioned in practice as commercial breaks. Soccer has historically resisted the kind of structured stoppage time that American broadcasters use to sell advertising. The continuous flow of the game is part of its identity and part of why global broadcast deals for soccer have been structured differently than American sports properties.

The hydration breaks changed that, at least partially. Whether they were implemented primarily for player welfare or primarily because a predictable stoppage at roughly the same point in each half is extremely valuable to broadcasters is a question the sport's governing bodies would answer one way and a cynic would answer another way. The practical effect was a stoppage that looked like a timeout and functioned like one commercially. For a new fan watching the sport for the first time in a major tournament, it stood out as something worth noticing.

Ticket Prices and the Access Question

The ticket price complaints about this World Cup were loud and broadly justified. The prices FIFA set for matches were high enough that a significant portion of the complaints came not from casual fans priced out of a single game but from dedicated supporters who had planned to attend multiple matches and found the total cost prohibitive. The secondary market made this worse, as it always does for major events with constrained supply and high demand.

This matters beyond the obvious fan access concern because the World Cup being held in the United States was supposed to grow the sport here. The argument for bringing the tournament to a market that has historically been resistant to soccer fandom was that exposure at this scale would convert casual observers into engaged fans. That argument is harder to make when the price of attendance excludes a significant portion of the population you are trying to convert.

I went to one match. I am glad I went. I am also aware that the price I paid for that ticket was not trivial, and I am a law student with some disposable income this summer. For a family trying to attend together, the math looked considerably worse.

FIFA and Commercialization

The FIFA president's public commentary during this World Cup about exploring options for further commercializing the product was the piece of this that interested me most from a business and legal standpoint. Soccer's governing structure is genuinely unusual compared to American professional sports leagues. FIFA operates as a nonprofit but generates revenue at a scale that puts it in the same conversation as the largest for-profit sports enterprises in the world. The tension between that nonprofit status, the commercial ambitions of the organization, and the interests of the national federations and clubs that make up its membership is one of the more interesting structural problems in sports business right now.

What expanded commercialization actually looks like is unclear. Whether it means broadcast rights restructuring, new tournament formats designed to generate more inventory, greater integration of sponsor branding into the game itself, or something else is not specified in the public discussion. But the direction of travel is obvious. The organization that controls the most-watched sporting event in the world is looking at that asset and concluding it can be monetized more aggressively. From a pure business standpoint that is a defensible conclusion. From the standpoint of a new fan who was just converted by the atmosphere of the thing, it is worth watching carefully.

Three Months In

Tottenham is losing a lot. School started back and the early kick-off times for Premier League matches are not compatible with a first-year law student's schedule in the way I optimistically imagined they might be in June. I have not watched as much as I intended.

I am still in, though. The Atlanta match did what the first MLS game did: it gave me a reason to pay attention to something I was not paying attention to before. The sport is more interesting than I expected from the outside. The business around it is more interesting than the sport in certain ways, which I suspect says something about where I am in my career right now.

I will keep watching. At some point Spurs will win something and I will feel vindicated. I have been a Minnesota sports fan long enough to know how to wait.

Jay Haws is a JD/MBA candidate at Wake Forest University School of Law, Class of 2027. He supports Tottenham, Atletico Madrid, and Borussia Dortmund and is choosing to remain optimistic about all three.
